\subsection{Proofs}
\subsubsection*{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:invariance}}
A strictly increasing transformation preserves all order relations and all ties within a segment. It therefore preserves average ranks, the indicator sequences, their empirical variances, and the smoothed periodograms. Every regression and bootstrap quantity is a function of these responses, the covariates, and the weights, proving the first statement. For the second statement, duplicating a row $r$ times and assigning weight $w_{ij}/r$ to each copy preserves its total contribution to the weighted cross-products and weighted response products. Thus $Q_i$, $\widehat Q$, $\widehat\beta$, and $\widehat U_i$ are unchanged. Substitution into \eqref{eq:sandwich} with $c_N=1$ proves the covariance statement. \hfill$\square$

\subsubsection*{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}}
The population normal equations give $\E U_{i,N}(s)=0$. The uniform moment bounds imply triangular-array laws of large numbers, so $\widehat Q_N-Q_N\to0$ in probability and $\widehat Q_N^{-1}\to Q^{-1}$ as $N\to\infty$. The sample normal equations yield
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proof-normal}
 \widehat\beta_N(s)-\beta_N(s)
 =\widehat Q_N^{-1}\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N U_{i,N}(s).
\end{equation}
For the finite stack of scores, (A2) bounds the Lindeberg truncated second moments at any threshold proportional to $\sqrt N$ by $CN^{-\eta/2}$. Covariance convergence therefore gives the triangular-array central limit theorem, including singular limits. Equation~\eqref{eq:linear} follows from \eqref{eq:proof-normal} and Slutsky's theorem, and $\max_s\|\widehat\beta_N(s)-\beta_N(s)\|=\Op(N^{-1/2})$.

For covariance consistency, $\widehat U_{i,N}(s)=U_{i,N}(s)-Q_{i,N}\{\widehat\beta_N(s)-\beta_N(s)\}$. Thus
$N^{-1}\sum_i\|\widehat U_{i,N}(s)-U_{i,N}(s)\|^2\leq\|\widehat\beta_N(s)-\beta_N(s)\|^2N^{-1}\sum_i\|Q_{i,N}\|^2=\op(1)$, uniformly on the finite grid. The uniform $2+\eta$ moments give uniform $1+\eta/2$ moments for the score products and hence $N^{-1}\sum_iU_{i,N}(s)U_{i,N}(s')\trans-\Omega_N(s,s')\to0$ in probability. Cauchy--Schwarz controls the cross-terms from replacing $U_{i,N}$ by $\widehat U_{i,N}$. Since $M_N\geq N$ and $p$ is fixed, $c_N\to1$. Substitution into \eqref{eq:sandwich} proves the assertion. \hfill$\square$

\subsubsection*{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:bootstrap}}
Conditionally on the data, $\sqrt N(\widehat\beta_N^*-\widehat\beta_N)$ is a sum of independent mean-zero vectors with covariance blocks $\widehat Q_N^{-1}\{N^{-1}\sum_i\widehat U_{i,N}(s)\widehat U_{i,N}(s')\trans\}\widehat Q_N^{-1}$. These converge to $V(s,s')$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}. A union bound and (A2) give $\max_{i,s}\|U_{i,N}(s)\|/\sqrt N\to0$ and $\max_i\|Q_{i,N}\|/\sqrt N\to0$ in probability as $N\to\infty$. Together with $\max_s\|\widehat\beta_N(s)-\beta_N(s)\|=\Op(N^{-1/2})$, these bounds establish the conditional Lindeberg condition for the residual score stack. Its conditional Gaussian limit is therefore the limit in Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}.

For the bootstrap denominator, write $\Delta_N^*(s)=\widehat\beta_N^*(s)-\widehat\beta_N(s)$, which is conditionally $\Op(N^{-1/2})$. Because $\xi_i^2=1$, the leading term in $N^{-1}\sum_i\widehat U_{i,N}^*(s)\widehat U_{i,N}^*(s')\trans$ is exactly $N^{-1}\sum_i\widehat U_{i,N}(s)\widehat U_{i,N}(s')\trans$. The mean squared residual-score correction is bounded by $\max_s\|\Delta_N^*(s)\|^2N^{-1}\sum_i\|Q_{i,N}\|^2=\op(1)$ in conditional probability. Cauchy--Schwarz controls the cross-terms, so the bootstrap covariance converges to $V$ uniformly over the finite grid.

Condition (A3) and the continuous mapping theorem give the same limiting maximum of absolute standardized Gaussian coordinates for $Z_N$ and $T_N^*$. This maximum has a continuous distribution, strictly increasing on $(0,\infty)$: on the Gaussian support the maximum absolute coordinate is a norm, and every annulus between positive radii has positive probability. Weak convergence thus gives uniform distributional convergence in \eqref{eq:bootstrap-valid}. Quantile consistency gives coverage and null rejection probability. Under the stated separated alternatives, the largest absolute contrast difference is bounded away from zero, while its standard error is $\Op(N^{-1/2})$. The observed maximum diverges and the bootstrap critical value is bounded in probability. \hfill$\square$

\subsubsection*{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}}
The two estimators have the same design matrix, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oracle-difference}
 \sqrt N\{\widehat\beta_N(s)-\widetilde\beta_N^0(s)\}
 =\widehat Q_N^{-1}\left[
 \sqrt N\E A_{i,N}(s)+\frac1{\sqrt N}
 \sum_{i=1}^N\{A_{i,N}(s)-\E A_{i,N}(s)\}\right].
\end{equation}
The first term in \eqref{eq:oracle-difference} tends to zero by \eqref{eq:transfer-conditions}. Independence across subjects and its second condition make the mean squared norm of the second term tend to zero. The fixed grid and $\widehat Q_N^{-1}=\Op(1)$ give the asserted uniform estimator difference as $N\to\infty$.

The difference of observed and oracle residual scores is $A_{i,N}-Q_{i,N}(\widehat\beta_N-\widetilde\beta_N^0)$. Conditions \eqref{eq:transfer-conditions} imply $\E\max_s\|A_{i,N}(s)\|^2\to0$. Markov's inequality and $N^{-1}\sum_i\|Q_{i,N}\|^2=\Op(1)$ show that the mean squared score difference tends to zero in probability. Cauchy--Schwarz and oracle covariance consistency give equivalence of the sandwich covariances. Couple the observed and oracle bootstraps using the same subject multipliers. The conditional mean squared norm of their normalized score-sum difference is bounded by this mean squared score difference, hence is $\op(1)$. The covariance argument also applies after bootstrap refitting. Theorems~\ref{thm:clt}--\ref{thm:bootstrap}, applied to the oracle scores, then give the coverage assertion. \hfill$\square$

\subsubsection*{Log transformation and proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:growth}}
The following elementary bounds quantify the effect of taking logarithms.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:logbias}
Let $\widehat S,S\geq0$, $\epsilon>0$, and $d=\widehat S-S$, where $S$ is measurable with respect to a sigma-field $\mathcal H$ and $\E(d^2\mid\mathcal H)<\infty$. For $e=\log(\widehat S+\epsilon)-\log(S+\epsilon)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logbias}
 |\E(e\mid\mathcal H)|
 \leq\frac{|\E(d\mid\mathcal H)|}{\epsilon}
       +\frac{\E(d^2\mid\mathcal H)}{2\epsilon^2},
 \qquad
 \E(e^2\mid\mathcal H)\leq\frac{\E(d^2\mid\mathcal H)}{\epsilon^2}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
For $g(x)=\log(x+\epsilon)$ on $[0,\infty)$, $|g'|\leq\epsilon^{-1}$ and $|g''|\leq\epsilon^{-2}$. Taylor's formula gives $|g(S+d)-g(S)-d/(S+\epsilon)|\leq d^2/(2\epsilon^2)$, and the mean value theorem gives $|e|\leq|d|/\epsilon$. Taking conditional expectations proves \eqref{eq:logbias}. \hfill$\square$

To prove the corollary, put $B_N=b_N/\epsilon+v_N/(2\epsilon^2)$ and $\mu_{i,N}(s)=\E\{A_{i,N}(s)\mid\mathcal H_{i,N}\}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:logbias} and $\sum_jw_{ij,N}=1$ give $\|\mu_{i,N}(s)\|^2\leq B_N^2\operatorname{tr}(Q_{i,N})$. The absolute covariance row-sum bound gives
\[
 \E\{\|A_{i,N}(s)-\mu_{i,N}(s)\|^2\mid\mathcal H_{i,N}\}
 \leq Cv_N\sum_jw_{ij,N}^2\|x_{ij,N}\|^2
 \leq\frac{Cv_N}{\underline m_N}\operatorname{tr}(Q_{i,N}).
\]
Indeed, expand the squared norm and use $2w_jw_k|x_j\trans x_k|\leq w_j^2\|x_j\|^2+w_k^2\|x_k\|^2$. Condition (A2) bounds $\E\operatorname{tr}(Q_{i,N})$. Total variance and the finite grid therefore imply
\[
 \max_s\|\E A_{i,N}(s)\|\leq CB_N,
 \qquad
 \E\max_s\|A_{i,N}(s)-\E A_{i,N}(s)\|^2
 \leq C\{B_N^2+v_N/\underline m_N\}.
\]
Substituting these bounds into \eqref{eq:oracle-difference} proves \eqref{eq:growth-rate}. If $\sqrt N(b_N+v_N)\to0$, then $B_N\sqrt N\to0$ and $v_N/\underline m_N\to0$, proving \eqref{eq:transfer-conditions}. \hfill$\square$

\subsubsection*{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:ratios}}
The bandwidth choice gives $b_N+v_N=O(n_N^{-2/3})$. Taking $\underline m_N=cm_N$ in \eqref{eq:growth-rate}, for sufficiently large $N$, yields
\[
 \max_{s\in\mathcal G}\sqrt N\|\widehat\beta_N(s)-\widetilde\beta_N^0(s)\|
 =\Op\left(\sqrt N n_N^{-2/3}+n_N^{-1/3}m_N^{-1/2}\right)=\op(1).
\]
Indeed, $\rho>0$, including $\rho=\infty$, makes $n_N/N$ bounded away from zero, so the first term is $O(N^{-1/6})$; the second vanishes as $m_N,n_N\to\infty$. Since $\sqrt N(b_N+v_N)\to0$, Corollary~\ref{cor:growth} and the oracle versions of Theorems~\ref{thm:clt}--\ref{thm:bootstrap} give the remaining assertions. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{Simulation models and estimands}
For the regression-surface experiment, generate independent standard normal variables $z_i,a_i^0,u_i,v_i$ and put $a_i=0.5z_i+a_i^0$. The segment covariate is $r_{ij}=0.6a_i+0.2z_i+0.5v_i+\nu_{ij}$, with independent standard normal $\nu_{ij}$. The regression weights are $1/m_i$. Let $g_1(f)=\exp[-\{(f-0.08)/0.035\}^2/2]$ and $g_2(f)=\exp[-\{(f-0.21)/0.045\}^2/2]$. The coefficient surfaces are $b_a(f,\tau)=\delta(2\tau-1)^2\{g_1(f)-0.6g_2(f)\}$, $b_z(f,\tau)=0.2\cos(2\pi f)$, and $b_r(f,\tau)=0.4\{1+0.5(\tau-0.5)\}g_2(f)$.

Define six basis functions $B_{kl}(f,\tau)=6^{-1/2}\cos(\pi k\tau)\cos(2\pi lf/0.6)$, using pairs $(k,l)$ in $\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,1),(2,1),(0,2),(1,2)\}$. Enumerate these pairs in the displayed order and write their basis functions as $B_r$, $r=1,\ldots,6$. A subject random surface is $b_i=0.55\sum_{r=1}^6c_{ir}B_r$, where $c_{i1}=u_i$ and the remaining coefficients are independent standard normals. Let $\eta_{ij}=0.35\sum_{r=1}^6d_{ijr}B_r$, with independent standard normal $d_{ijr}$. Set $e_{i1}=\eta_{i1}$ and $e_{ij}=0.4e_{i,j-1}+\sqrt{0.84}\eta_{ij}$ for subsequent segments. The response is $Y_{ij}=a_i b_a+z_i b_z+r_{ij}b_r+b_i+e_{ij}$. The model is evaluated at five quantiles $\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$ and 30 equally spaced frequencies from 0.015 to 0.30. The true exposure coefficient is $b_a$ on this grid.

Balanced designs use a common segment count. In the informative-count experiment, $m_i=12$ when $a_i u_i>0$ and $m_i=4$ otherwise. Subject normalization preserves the stated projection coefficient because the weighted design--error cross-moment is zero. Weighting all segments equally instead weights the population by $m_i$ and changes the exposure projection. Its reported coverage is evaluated against the subject-weighted target, so a failure of coverage in that experiment can reflect an estimand difference as well as covariance estimation.

For the time-series experiments, the exposure takes values $-1/2$ and $1/2$ in balanced groups. Independent standard normal $z_i$ and $u_i$ determine $\phi_i=0.08+0.84\operatorname{expit}(\delta a_i+0.4z_i+0.9u_i)$, where $\operatorname{expit}(v)=(1+e^{-v})^{-1}$. Conditional on $\phi_i$, the Gaussian autoregression is $X_{ijt}=\phi_iX_{ij,t-1}+\sqrt{1-\phi_i^2}\,\varepsilon_{ijt}$, initialized from its stationary $N(0,1)$ distribution. The stochastic-volatility process is $X_{ijt}=\exp\{(H_{ijt}-\sigma_H^2/2)/2\}\varepsilon_{ijt}$, where $\sigma_H=1.5$ and $H_{ijt}=\phi_i H_{ij,t-1}+\sigma_H\sqrt{1-\phi_i^2}\,\zeta_{ijt}$ is initialized from $N(0,\sigma_H^2)$. The innovations are independent standard normals. Segments are independent conditional on subject effects, and remain dependent marginally because they share those effects. Regression adjusts for $z_i$.

In the volatility model, $\E(X_{ijt}\mid H_{ijt})=0$, $\E X_{ijt}^2=1$, and $\cov(X_{ijt},X_{ij,t-\ell})=0$ for every nonzero $\ell$. The population ordinary spectrum is therefore flat for every exposure value. Also, $\ind(X_{ijt}\leq0)=\ind(\varepsilon_{ijt}\leq0)$, so the population median indicator process is independent over time. Other quantile indicator processes can depend on volatility persistence. Equality of these population spectra does not imply equality of expected finite-window log periodograms, which can retain persistence-dependent transformation bias. The log transformation contributes the second-moment term in the bound in \eqref{eq:logbias}.

Ordinary spectral regression uses the log of a Gaussian-smoothed periodogram of the demeaned signal, retaining its amplitude scale. It uses the same frequencies and smoothing bandwidth as the quantile analysis. The median-only method uses $\tau=0.5$ and takes its maximum only over frequency. The independent-segment method uses the quantile responses but treats each segment as a separate cluster. These procedures have different response targets; comparisons under nonzero effects concern their sensitivity to the specified data-generating change.
